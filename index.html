Serving alcohol is
a legal act.
Here's the law bit.

This training isn't about pouring the drinks, or selling, or how we are with the residents. This is the part where you and we can be fined if we sell to a resident who is underage or drunk — and where we could end up having our licence taken away.

The general rule: we cannot sell alcohol to anyone who is not over eighteen, and we must always ask for ID if we feel somebody looks under twenty-five. From now on, we are always cautious.

Free food, paid alcohol — and the trap in that We give the food away and residents pay for the drinks, or we serve the drinks ourselves. The alcohol is the licensable part, so all of these rules attach to it and none of them attach to the buffet. And if an event ever includes a welcome drink, that drink is still a sale of alcohol — it still needs an age check, and it still can't go to an under-18.
"We're serving it, so it's free" is not a way out Where we provide the drinks rather than charge for them, anything paid for by the resident in any form — a ticket, an event charge, a service charge — makes it a sale in law. And even where a drink is genuinely free with nothing paid at all, an under-18 still must not be drinking alcohol on these premises, and letting them is an offence in itself.

So the simple rule, and our policy: treat every drink you hand over as a sale. Same age check, same refusal, same log entry, whether money changes hands or not. No excuses.

And one thing that is fine: because we are not a restaurant, an adult may buy a drink and carry it to another adult. That is normal. What we do not have is the restaurant exception that lets an adult buy beer, wine or cider for a 16 or 17 year old with a table meal.

01 — Licensed like a pub on the high street

The Club, West Lounge and The Podium hold a premises licence, and we have to follow exactly the same rules as any pub. A licence is permission, not a right: it is granted against four objectives set by the Act, and it can be reviewed and taken away.

Where we are is not a loophole "It's private", "they live here", "it's a members' area", "the food's free", "it's only a residents' night" — none of that changes anything. Same licence, same rules, same consequences as the pub down the road.

The four licensing objectives — and what each one means here

  1. The prevention of crime and disorder. Not brawls — nobody expects a fight at a residents' screening. Here it means not serving somebody who is already drunk, logging what happens, and being able to show the police we run a tight operation.
  2. Public safety. Capacity, exits, glass, spills, and people getting home safely. Our building, our stairwells, our lifts.
  3. The prevention of public nuisance. Noise, drinks drifting outside the boundary, and how the night ends.
  4. The protection of children from harm. Age checks, children in areas where we are serving, and speaking up when something looks wrong.

Learn these four. They are fixed by the Act — we don't get to pick which ones apply to us — and a licensing or police officer can ask you what they are. "I don't know" is itself a finding about how the premises is run.

Objective four affects us more than most venues

Families live in this building. Children use The Podium every day and sit in The Club and West Lounge doing homework. Two of the three spaces we serve in are somewhere children already are before we open a bar in them. That makes age checks and children-on-premises rules the sharpest end of our licence, not a formality.

And objective three is about the people upstairs

A Podium BBQ or a party is directly below and beside people's bedrooms. Volume, glass, and how cleanly a night ends are all licence business — a single persistent complaint from one resident can trigger a review of the whole licence.

02 — Who is actually allowed to sell alcohol

Every single sale of alcohol has to be made or authorised by a personal licence holder. That's the chain of authority you're standing in.

Premises licencePermission for this building to sell alcohol, at set times, in set areas, subject to conditions.
Designated Premises Supervisor (DPS)The named person responsible for day-to-day supervision of alcohol sales here — Tony Jacobs. Must hold a personal licence.
Personal licenceHeld by an individual after a qualification and DBS check. Authorises alcohol sales.
YouYou don't need your own personal licence to serve — you sell under the DPS's written authorisation. That authorisation is what makes your sale lawful.
Selling with no authorisation is a criminal offence Carrying on a licensable activity without authorisation s.136 risks an unlimited fine and up to six months' imprisonment — for the individual as well as the business.

What that means in practice

  • Only serve alcohol if you are on the authorised list and have completed this training. Both are recorded.
  • Only on an authorised event. We don't sell alcohol day to day. If there is no event on tonight, there is no lawful sale to make — whatever the fridge has in it.
  • Alcohol may only be sold in the licensed areas and within the licensed hours. Not in the lobby, not in the lifts, not in the gym, not at 01:30 because a party is going well.
  • On the Podium, know where the licensed boundary runs before you open. Drinks stay inside it.
  • If the DPS and management are off site, the rules do not relax. There is no "acting" version of the law.
  • Staff under 18 may only make a sale of alcohol if it is specifically approved by a responsible person, for that sale s.153. In practice at OWP: if you are under 18, you do not serve alcohol.

03 — Age: the offences, and who gets punished

This is the one that ends careers and licences. Read the "who" column carefully — several of these land on you personally, not on the company.

OffenceWhat it isWho is liable
Selling alcohol to a child s.146Selling to anyone under 18. It doesn't matter whether you knew, only whether you took reasonable steps.The person who made the sale — an unlimited fine and a criminal record
Allowing the sale s.147Knowingly allowing someone else to sell to an under-18.Supervisors, DPS, anyone who let it happen

The restaurant exception — know it, because it will be quoted at you In a restaurant, a 16 or 17 year old may drink beer, wine or cider with a table meal, in an area set aside for meals, if it is bought by an adult 18 or over and they are accompanied by that adult s.150(4). Not spirits. Not at the bar. Not under 16, ever.

It is a real rule, so you won't be being lied to when a resident quotes it. It is just not our rule.
We are not a restaurant, so it does not apply to us We serve buffets, screenings and BBQs — not table meals in an area set aside for meals. So work on this basis: at our events nobody under 18 drinks alcohol. Not with food, not with a parent, not at a table. Expect a resident to try it one day, usually a parent with a 16 or 17 year old at a screening. Under 18 means no drink, and the supervisor on duty will back you.

Your defence, if it ever comes to it

The law gives a due diligence defence: that you believed the person was 18 or over and either you had taken all reasonable steps to check, or nobody could reasonably have told. In real life that defence is only as good as your evidence — the ID you actually inspected, the refusals log, the till prompt, this training record. Do it properly and you're protected. Guess, and you're not.

04 — Checking ID properly

Every premises licence carries a mandatory condition requiring an age verification policy. Ours is Challenge 25: if they look under 25, you ask. No judgement call, no exceptions, no "I'll let this one go".

Know today's date. Then know the date that makes somebody 18. 1. Know today's date, and write it at the top of the refusals log when you set the bar up.   2. Count back 18 years — that gives the latest date of birth that is old enough to be served.   3. Count back 25 years — born after that date, ask for ID every time, whoever they are.

Both dates move every single day. Work them out for the date of your event and write them where the whole bar team can see them.

ID we accept

  • Passport
  • Photocard driving licence
  • A PASS-accredited proof-of-age card (look for the PASS hologram)
  • HM Forces identity card with photo
  • Biometric residence permit / immigration card
  • National identity card issued by an EEA state

The mandatory condition requires photo ID showing a date of birth and carrying a holographic mark or an ultraviolet feature. Digital ID is only acceptable where our own policy says so — if you're not certain it's on our list, treat it as no ID.

Do

  • Take the card in your hand and look at it in decent light
  • Read the date of birth and do the maths — never read the age off somebody's face
  • Check the photo is them: ears, nose, bone structure, not the haircut
  • Feel for edges that have been split, letters that don't line up, a laminate that's lifting
  • Tilt it for the hologram
  • Serve pleasantly once it checks out. Being thanked for being thorough is normal

Don't

  • Accept a photo or screenshot of an ID on a phone
  • Accept a photocopy, a bank card, a student card, or a gym pass
  • Accept "my mate's got my ID upstairs"
  • Let a group hold you up — check the person buying, and anyone the drink is clearly for
  • Skip the check because you served them last week
  • Accept an ID somebody else in the group hands you
The OWP trap: familiarity You will serve the same few hundred residents and their families over and over. You'll know faces, names, flat numbers. The under-18 who gets served in a place like this is almost never a stranger with a fake ID — it's someone's teenager whose face you recognise from the lift. Recognising somebody is not evidence of their age.

If the ID looks fake or isn't theirs

  1. Refuse the sale, calmly. You don't need to prove anything or accuse anyone.
  2. Don't snatch, wrestle or chase. Don't get into a debate.
  3. Log it in the refusals register with a description, and tell your supervisor and the DPS the same shift.
  4. If they become abusive or refuse to leave, follow the venue's incident procedure and call for support.

05 — Saying no, and writing it down

You are always allowed to refuse a sale. There is no legal right to be served. Refusing is the safe answer — you can never be prosecuted for a sale you didn't make.

A refusal that doesn't turn into an argument

  • Be quick and matter of fact. Hesitating invites negotiation.
  • Put it on the rule, not the person: "I can't serve this without ID — Challenge 25, it's a condition of our licence."
  • Offer something instead: a soft drink, food, tap water, "come back with your passport and it's no problem".
  • Say it once, then stop explaining. Repeating the same line calmly beats a better argument.
  • Hand off to a supervisor if it escalates. That's not failing.
  • Tell the rest of the bar team, so the same person doesn't get served two feet away thirty seconds later.
The refusals register is our best evidence A log full of entries proves a premises that checks. An empty log tells a licensing officer that nobody ever asked anybody anything. Fill it in at the time, not at the end of the night.

06 — Drunk customers

Serving somebody who is already drunk is a separate criminal offence from anything to do with age, and it's the one most often ignored. It applies even to a resident, even at a private function, even when they're the reason the bar is busy.

Selling to a drunk person s.141Selling or attempting to sell alcohol to somebody who is drunk, or allowing it. A fine — and it's your sale.
Buying for a drunk person s.142Obtaining or attempting to obtain alcohol for somebody who is drunk. That covers the friend at the bar buying the round.
Failure to leave s.143A person who is drunk or disorderly commits an offence if they don't leave when asked, or try to enter.
Allowing disorderly conduct s.140Knowingly allowing disorderly conduct on the premises.

What you're looking for

  • Slurred, repetitive or unusually loud speech
  • Unsteady on their feet, swaying, using furniture to navigate, difficulty with stairs or the lift
  • Can't focus, glazed, struggling to pay or handle a card
  • Fumbling, spilling, dropping glasses
  • Personality change — aggression, tearfulness, over-familiarity, no sense of volume
  • Ordering doubles at pace, or a round no group could reasonably drink
How to cut someone off in a building they live in Quietly, one to one, no audience: "I can't serve you any more tonight — but let me get you some water and something to eat." Never announce it across the bar. Never make it about their character. Then tell your supervisor and log it, because the next person on the bar needs to know.

Keep an eye on where they go next. If somebody is very drunk or unwell, their welfare is the priority — get help, don't just move them off the premises. Free drinking water must be available on request; use it.

07 — Children on the premises, and screening nights

Children being present is normal here — they're in The Club and West Lounge every day. The catch is that the rules change underneath them the moment a space turns into an event with a bar.

The room changes character, and so do the rules The Club at 4pm is a café — not premises being used primarily for the supply of alcohol, so an under-16 can sit there alone doing homework quite lawfully. The same room at 8pm on a residents' night, with a bar running, is. The law doesn't care that it's the same carpet. Walk the room and deal with unaccompanied under-16s before the bar opens, not once you're pouring.
  • Unaccompanied under-16s must not be on premises being used exclusively or primarily for the supply of alcohol s.145. During a drinks-led event, an under-16 needs an adult 18 or over with them.
  • Between midnight and 05:00, an accompanied child must not be on premises open for the supply of alcohol for consumption there.
  • Sending a child to buy alcohol s.152 is an offence — including a parent sending their child to the bar with a card and an order.
  • Watch the table, not just the till. If a round is bought by an adult and lands in front of a 15-year-old, that's an offence in progress and it's yours to stop.
  • Children in areas that aren't primarily for alcohol — a family event on The Podium, a residents' film night — are fine. The alcohol rules still apply to who drinks.
Safeguarding overrides everything If you're worried about a child — unsupervised late, distressed, with an adult who doesn't seem to be theirs, being given alcohol — tell your supervisor and the DPS straight away. Don't investigate it yourself and don't decide it's not your business. Protecting children from harm is one of the four objectives our licence stands on.

Our own premises licence may add conditions on children beyond the statutory minimum — for example restricted areas or times. Read the conditions once the licence is issued and ask if anything is unclear.

Screening nights: the 3 × 8 metre screen brings its own condition

Showing a film is regulated entertainment in its own right, and any licence authorising it carries a mandatory condition about children that has nothing to do with alcohol:

Admission must match the film's age classification Where a film is shown, admission of children has to be restricted in accordance with the classification given to that film. A 15 means nobody under 15 comes in — a parent saying it's fine does not change that. Know the certificate before the night, put it on the invitation, and check ages at the door rather than halfway through.
  • Lights down means you can't see who's drinking what. All your age checks have to happen at the bar, at the point of sale. A dark room is a reason to be stricter, not more relaxed.
  • Keep the bar area lit well enough to actually read a date of birth off a card.
  • A family film afternoon and a residents' drinks night are two different events. We have to choose which it is — and say so on the invitation.
  • Screening rights for the film itself are a separate issue from the premises licence — but they land on the same event, so check before anything goes on that screen.

Screenings in West Lounge are our highest-risk event: a dark room, a buffet, families, and a bar. Everything else on our licence gets tested on those nights.

08 — The conditions we operate under

Some conditions come from the licence itself; some are mandatory on every premises licence in England and Wales. Breaching any of them is a breach of the licence.

Mandatory conditions — these apply to us whatever the licence says

  • No irresponsible promotions. No drinking games or rewards for drinking quickly, no unlimited or unspecified quantities for a fixed price, no promotions tied to sporting events that encourage bad behaviour, nothing that appeals to under-18s.
  • No dispensing alcohol directly into the mouth — no shot funnels, no "head back" pouring, unless it's for someone unable to drink unaided.
  • Free potable water must be available to customers on request.
  • An age verification policy requiring photo ID with date of birth and a hologram or UV feature. The DPS must ensure staff are trained on it — that's this session, and it's recorded.
  • Smaller measures must be available and customers made aware of them: half a pint of beer or cider, a 125ml glass of wine, a 25ml or 35ml spirit. Don't only offer the large one.
  • No sales below the permitted price (broadly, duty plus VAT).

Legal measures — get these wrong and it's a weights and measures offence

DrinkLawful quantities
Draught beer & cider⅓ pint, ½ pint, or multiples of ½ pint
Still wine by the glass125ml, 175ml, 250ml, or multiples
Gin, rum, vodka, whisky25ml or 35ml, or multiples of that same measure

Use the stamped glass or the measure. Never free-pour spirits. Don't mix 25ml and 35ml on the same site.

The conditions the police have put on our licence

These three are specific to us. They are not general good practice — they are conditions on the licence, so failing to do them is a breach of the licence in exactly the same way as serving an under-18.

  1. A guest register at every event. All guests on event days must sign in, recording full name and contact details.
  2. A risk assessment for SIA door supervisors. At all times, the premises licence holder will conduct a risk assessment to determine the number of SIA door supervisors required. The assessment must be retained on site for 12 months and made available to the local authority or an officer of the Metropolitan Police Service on request.
  3. The Lounge closes at midnight, Monday to Sunday.
Every event, not just the big ones A risk assessment is needed before every event — an OPDC lunch as much as a residents' party. We have a simple sheet that builds the assessment once you fill in a few facts, so there is no reason to skip it.

We can be checked at any time, and we keep every assessment for a year after the event. An assessment written up afterwards is worth nothing: do it before, and file it.
The register is a legal record, so treat it like one Sign guests in as they arrive, not from memory at the end. Names must be legible and contact details real. Because it holds personal data, it is kept securely, used only for licensing and safety, and not left lying on the bar for the next guest to read.

Two practical consequences of the midnight condition: the Lounge closes at midnight whatever the mood in the room, and it lines up with the rule that even an accompanied child must not be on premises open for the supply of alcohol between midnight and 05:00. Plan the end of the night backwards from midnight, not towards it.

Where the licensed area ends — the Podium problem

Outdoor events make the boundary of the licensed area a live question rather than a line on a plan. Before service starts you need to know where it runs, and drinks have to stay inside it.

  • A resident wandering back towards the lifts with a bottle is taking alcohol away from the premises. That's an off-sale, and off-sales are only lawful if our licence permits them.
  • If off-sales aren't permitted, drinks finish inside the licensed area. Say it at the start of the event, not at the end.
  • Glass outdoors, near a pool of residents including children, is a public safety issue as much as a housekeeping one.
  • Volume and dispersal: flats overlook the Podium on every side. How quietly a night ends is licence business, and one persistent complainant can trigger a review.

Also on our licence — check the document when it's issued

  • Permitted hours for alcohol sales, and hours for regulated entertainment including film
  • Which areas are licensed, whether the Podium is included, and whether off-sales are permitted at all
  • CCTV, incident log, refusals log and staff training record requirements
  • Conditions on noise, outdoor use and dispersal
  • Anything specific to screenings or to children in The Club and West Lounge
We have worked hard to get this licence Let's not be silly, or so friendly, that we make the one mistake that loses it.

What getting it wrong actually costs: An unlimited fine and a criminal record for the individual server. An unlimited fine for the company. Suspension of alcohol sales for up to three months after two under-18 sales in three months. A licence review triggered by police, environmental health or a single resident — which can add conditions, cut our hours, remove the DPS, or revoke the licence altogether. A closed bar affects every resident in this building.

Before every event — and the records we keep

Before every event

  • Confirm the event is authorised and that we are within the licensed hours.
  • Confirm the DPS has been notified, and that everybody serving has been trained.
  • Name the trained supervisor on duty. They have the final say on any refusal.
  • Brief the team on the licensed boundary — especially on the Podium — and whether drinks may leave it.
  • Complete the SIA risk assessment and file it — every event, no exceptions. Kept on site for 12 months.
  • Set up the guest register so every guest signs in with full name and contact details as they arrive.
  • Check the refusals log is out, on the bar, with a pen.
  • Walk the room and deal with any unaccompanied under-16s before the bar opens.
  • For screenings: confirm the film's age classification, and who is checking ages at the door.

Records to keep

  • A signed training record for every person serving, with the date and the version of this material.
  • The DPS's written authorisation list — who may serve alcohol.
  • The refusals and incident log, filled in at the time.
  • Evidence of refresher training before events, given how rarely we serve.
  • The SIA risk assessment for every event, kept for 12 months and producible to the council or the Met on request.
  • The guest register for every event, held securely.

Keep all of it with the licence. It is the first thing asked for at a review, and it is what proves we took reasonable steps.
