One West Point · Café licensing training · Licensing Act 2003

Selling alcohol in the café is a legal act.

This is not about how to pour a glass of wine. It is the version of the law that will affect us when we serve alcohol in the café: what you must check, when you must say no, and what happens to you personally if you get it wrong.

This is for everyone who takes payment or serves a drink. You have to complete it before you work in the café and serve alcohol.

WHERE The Club (café) · West Lounge · The Podium — one set of rules
WHEN 10:00–24:00, Monday to Sunday
DPS Tony Jacobs
VERSION Café 1.0
Eighteen to buy. Under twenty-five, ask for ID.

Those two sentences are most of your job. Everything below is how to do them properly, and what to do when somebody argues.

01What we are allowed to do

You are not working in a members' club with special rules. This is licensed premises, and it works like any café or pub that sells alcohol.

Hours10:00 to midnight, every day. Nothing before 10:00 and nothing after midnight — not one more, not for a regular, not for a resident.
WhereThe café, the West Lounge and the Podium. Treat all three as one licensed area with one set of rules.
On and off salesPeople may drink it here or take it away. Somebody buying a bottle of wine to take upstairs is a normal, lawful sale.
Who may serveOnly staff who have done this training and are on the DPS's authorisation list. If your name is not on it, you cannot take money for a drink.
So most things are a yes A glass of wine with a bagel at eleven in the morning is fine. A can taken back to a flat is fine. Two bottles bought at half past ten at night are fine. The job is not to be suspicious of everybody — it is to catch the specific things that are offences.
And these are always a no Anyone under 18. Anyone already drunk. Anyone buying for someone under 18. Anything before 10:00 or after midnight. Those four, every time, whoever is asking.

02The café in the daytime

This is the part residents will ask you about, so be clear on it. Selling alcohol does not turn the café into a bar, and almost nothing about how people use it in the daytime has to change.

What does not change — say this confidently The café is not premises used mainly for selling alcohol. It is a café that also sells alcohol. That distinction is what keeps everything else normal: children are still welcome, residents can still work here all afternoon, and an under-16 can still sit in on their own. The rule that keeps unaccompanied under-16s out of bars s.145 does not apply to us.

So if a parent asks whether their child can still come in, the answer is yes, exactly as before. If a resident asks whether the café is now "a pub", the answer is no — same café, same tables, same laptops, and now you can also buy a glass of wine with your bagel.

We can

  • Sell alcohol from 10:00 to midnight, any day, to anyone 18 or over
  • Sell it to take away as well as to drink here
  • Have children in all day, with or without an adult
  • Serve wine at a table with families sitting nearby
  • Serve a resident who takes it up to their flat
  • Serve hot drinks and food late — late night refreshment 23:00–24:00 is covered

We cannot

  • Sell before 10:00 or after midnight
  • Let anyone under 18 drink alcohol here — including a sip from a parent's glass
  • Let people bring their own alcohol in
  • Let an under-18 member of staff make an alcohol sale
  • Run any offer that encourages fast or unlimited drinking
  • Refuse somebody free tap water

Under-18s in the café

They can be here. They just cannot drink. Two things to watch, because both are offences and both look completely innocent:

  • The sip from a parent's glass. A parent letting their 15-year-old try their wine at a café table is an offence under s.150 — for the child, and for the adult who allows it. If you see it, say something politely. "Sorry, I can't let that happen in here, it's our licence" is enough.
  • The drink that travels. Watch the table, not just the till. If a round bought by an adult ends up in front of somebody under 18, that is an offence in progress and it is yours to stop.

Never send or let a child carry alcohol out for an adult ss.151, 152. And if something worries you about a child — distressed, with an adult who doesn't seem to be theirs, being given alcohol — tell the supervisor and the DPS straight away. Don't investigate it and don't decide it isn't your business.

If you are under 18 yourself

You can work in the café. You do not put an alcohol sale through the till. A person under 18 may only make a sale of alcohol if it is specifically approved for that sale by a responsible person s.153, so our policy is simpler: hand it to an authorised colleague. Nobody will mind, and it protects you.

Nobody brings their own

The café is licensed premises now, and that has an odd consequence: an under-18 drinking alcohol in here is an offence even if we did not sell it to them. Somebody's own bottle, brought from upstairs, still counts. That is one reason we do not allow drinks to be brought in — the other being that we cannot age-check or refuse a drink we never served. If a resident wants to celebrate, we will happily sell them something.

Three things that must be true every day

  • Smaller measures on offer, and customers told about them — see section 08. This one is a licence condition, not a courtesy.
  • Free drinking water available on request. Always, no questions.
  • The licence summary and the DPS's name on display, along with the Challenge 25 notices. If a notice falls down, put it back up — an officer walking in looks for these first.
And one thing that is judgement rather than law In the middle of the afternoon this is a family room. Keep alcohol service quiet and unremarkable — no bottles lined up on the counter, no wine glasses left standing on empty tables at four in the afternoon when the school run comes through. None of that is a legal requirement. It is how we keep residents comfortable and how we show the council we thought about children when they ask.

03Age: the rule and the offence

This is the part that carries a criminal record, and it lands on the person who took the money — not on the company.

You must not sell alcohol to anyone under 18 s.146. An unlimited fine, and it is your name on it. Whether you knew is not the test. Whether you took reasonable steps is.

Challenge 25 is how we take those steps. If somebody looks under 25, ask for photo ID. Every time. It is a condition of our licence, not a house preference.

Know today's date, and the date that makes somebody 18 1. Know today's date.  2. Count back 18 years — that is the latest date of birth old enough to be served.  3. Count back 25 years — born after that, ask for ID.

Work both out at the start of your shift and write them by the till. They move every single day.

There is no exception for us

You may hear that a 16 or 17 year old can drink beer or wine with a meal. That is a real rule — but it needs a table meal in an area set aside for meals, which means a restaurant. We serve coffee, bagels and crisps over a counter. So at OWP nobody under 18 drinks alcohol. No food, no parent, no table changes that.

Buying for somebody else

An adult buying a drink for another adult is completely normal. But an adult buying for an under-18 is proxy purchase s.149 — an offence for them, and an offence for you if you serve knowing where it is going. So watch who the drink is for, not just who is paying.

Off-sales too: handing a bottle to somebody under 18 to carry out is its own offence s.151.

04Checking ID in about five seconds

We accept

  • Passport
  • Photocard driving licence
  • PASS card (look for the hologram)
  • HM Forces photo ID
  • Biometric residence permit

Never accept

  • A photo of an ID on a phone
  • A photocopy
  • Bank cards, student cards, gym passes
  • "My ID's upstairs in the flat"
  • Somebody else in the group handing it to you

Take the card in your hand. Read the date of birth and do the maths — never read the age off somebody's face. Check the photo is them: bone structure, not the haircut. Feel for lifting laminate and split edges. Tilt it for the hologram.

The trap here is that you will know everybody You will serve the same residents every day. You will know faces, names, flat numbers. The under-18 who gets served in a place like this is almost never a stranger with a fake ID — it is somebody's teenager whose face you know from the queue. Recognising somebody is not evidence of their age. Being a resident is not proof of age either, and a resident record is not the photo ID our licence requires.

05Saying no

There is no legal right to be served, and you can never be prosecuted for a sale you did not make. Refusing is always the safe answer.

  • Be quick and matter of fact. Hesitating invites a negotiation.
  • Put it on the rule, not the person: "I can't serve this without ID — we ask everyone who looks under 25, it's a condition of our licence."
  • Offer something instead — a coffee, a soft drink, "bring your passport down and it's no problem".
  • Say it once, then stop explaining. Repeating the same line calmly beats finding a better argument.
  • Tell the rest of the team, so the same person isn't served two minutes later.
  • Hand off to the supervisor if it escalates. That isn't failing — they have the final say.
Write every refusal in the log, at the time Date and time, a description (no name needed), what was asked for, why you refused, what happened, and your name. A full log proves a café that checks. An empty log tells a licensing officer that nobody here ever asked anybody anything — and that log is your defence if a sale is ever questioned.

06Drunk customers

Selling alcohol to somebody who is already drunk is a separate offence s.141 — nothing to do with age. And obtaining alcohol for a drunk person is also an offence s.142, so serving their friend instead does not help either of you.

What you're looking for: slurred or repetitive speech, unsteady on their feet, glazed, struggling to handle a card, fumbling or spilling, personality change, or ordering at a pace nobody could keep up with.

How to do it in a building where they live Quietly, one to one, no audience: "I can't serve you any more today — let me get you a water and something to eat." Never announce it across the café. Never make it about their character. Then tell the supervisor and log it, so whoever is on next knows. Free drinking water must always be available on request — use it.

07Every drink is a sale — and looking after the stock

If a drink is ever comped, included in an event ticket, or handed over on the house, treat it exactly like a sale. Same age check, same refusal, same log entry, money or no money. Anything the customer pays in any form makes it a sale in law, and even a genuinely free drink cannot go to an under-18.

Opening and closing

  • Nothing sold before 10:00 or after midnight. Secure the display fridge outside those hours.
  • Alcohol is kept in the locked store when it isn't being sold. Named keyholders only.
  • Sign stock out to the counter at the start of a shift and back in at the end. Count the difference.
  • Deliveries go straight to the store — never left in the lobby, a corridor or an escape route.
  • First in, first out. Write the date on anything opened, and record wastage.
  • Use the stamped glass or the measure every time — see section 08. Free-pouring makes the count meaningless as well as being unlawful.

Stock control is not just about money. A clean count is how we can show every drink that left the fridge was rung through and age-checked.

08Measures, glasses and prices

Some drinks may only be sold in set quantities. This is weights and measures law, enforced by Trading Standards rather than the licensing team — and short measure is the thing they test for.

The easy part: bottles and cans are not affected at all If you sell a drink in the sealed container it came in — a bottle of beer, a can, a small single-serve bottle of wine, a whole bottle — none of these rules apply. You are selling the container, not a measure. If you are ever unsure, selling the sealed bottle or can is always safe.

The rules start the moment you pour a drink into a glass:

Sold by the glassThe only lawful quantities
Still wine125ml, 175ml, 250ml, or multiples of those
Draught beer and cider⅓ pint, ½ pint, or multiples of ½ pint
Gin, rum, vodka, whisky25ml or 35ml, or multiples of whichever we use

Nothing in between is allowed. There is no such thing as "a large one" or "a bit more than a small" — if somebody asks for a large glass of wine, that means 250ml.

If we pour wine by the glass

  • Use a stamped glass, filled to the line, or fill from a stamped measure. Never judge it by eye.
  • The 125ml has to be genuinely available, and customers have to be told it exists. That is a mandatory condition on every premises licence — so all three sizes go on the board with their prices, and you mention the small one rather than waiting to be asked.
  • Fill it properly. A 175ml glass with 155ml in it is short measure, and that is a straightforward offence. If somebody says it looks short, top it up without arguing.

If we pour spirits

  • We pick either 25ml or 35ml and use that one measure throughout the premises. We cannot run 25ml on some spirits and 35ml on others.
  • Always use the measure or the optic. Never free-pour — it is unlawful and it wrecks stock control.
  • A double is two of whatever our single is, and it gets charged as two.

What these rules do not cover

Sparkling wine and prosecco by the glass, fortified wines like port and sherry, cocktails, and liqueurs other than the four spirits named above. Sensible practice is to pick a house pour and stick to it anyway, so every customer gets the same drink for the same money.

Prices The price list has to show the price for the quantity we actually sell. So if wine goes on the board by the glass, each size gets its own price — not one price with the sizes left vague.

09What it costs if we get it wrong

YouUnlimited fine and a criminal record. Personally, in your name.
The companyUnlimited fine, and prosecution of the DPS for allowing it.
The premisesTwo unlawful sales to under-18s in three months and alcohol sales can be suspended for up to three months s.147A.
The licenceA review can add conditions, cut our hours, remove the DPS, or revoke the licence altogether.
We have worked hard to get this licence Let's not be silly, or so friendly, that we make the one mistake that loses it. Test purchases with an under-18 volunteer do happen, and a review can be triggered by the police, the council, or one resident.

Your protection is evidence: the ID you actually looked at, the refusals log, and this training record. Do it properly and you are covered. Guess, and you are not.
